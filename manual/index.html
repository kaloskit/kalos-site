00

Client Onboarding Procedure

Every new client follows the same onboarding sequence. This process exists to ensure the right clients are matched with the right program — and that every client begins their transformation with a clear, measurable goal and a fully prescribed plan.

Step 1 — PT Application

The prospective client submits a PT application covering general background information: training history, current lifestyle, availability, and initial goals. This gives the trainer context before any direct conversation takes place.

Step 2 — Schedule the Consultation

Upon receiving the application, reach out to the prospect promptly to schedule an in-person or virtual consultation. The consultation is the most important sales and assessment moment in the client relationship — treat it accordingly.

Step 3 — Conduct the Consultation

The consultation is a structured conversation using a standardized consultation form. Its purpose is to understand the client's full picture: their history, their motivations, their constraints, and their definition of success. Listen more than you speak.

Step 4 — Goal Evaluation

Evaluate the client's stated goals against their background and capacity. Where necessary, recalibrate expectations. All confirmed goals must be SMART — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound.

Identify any modifications needed to make the goal realistic
Confirm the goal in SMART terms with the client before proceeding
Document the confirmed goal in the client's profile

Step 5 — Program Prescription

Based on the confirmed goal, prescribe the complete program strategy across four components:

Training program. Define the training split, weekly frequency, and intensity approach appropriate to the client's goal and experience level.
Nutritional strategy. Prescribe the appropriate nutritional approach — meal planning, macro targets, or lifestyle adjustments — aligned to the client's transformation goal.
Cardio regimen. Define the cardio component: modality, frequency, and intensity, calibrated to support the training program without compromising recovery.
Supplements. Where appropriate, recommend evidence-based supplementation to support the client's goals.

Step 6 — Package Recommendation

Based on the client's confirmed goal, its time-bound nature, and the prescribed weekly training frequency, recommend the appropriate PT package. The package recommendation follows directly from the math of the goal.

Package recommendation example

A client's goal is to lose 16 lbs. A rate of 1 lb per week requires 16 weeks. The prescribed training frequency is 3 sessions per week. 3 × 16 = 48 sessions — the Transformation package is the correct recommendation.

Always tie the package recommendation to the client's own goal and timeline. The package is not a product you are selling — it is the vehicle for the result they have committed to.

The Macro-Cycle Training Block

Every client's program is structured around a four-phase macro-cycle that makes up one complete training block. The cycle rotates through Strength, Hypertrophy 1, Hypertrophy 2, and Hypertrophy Volume — each phase applying a different rep range, exercise selection, and intensity to drive optimal progression. The phase applies to the entire split at once — every training day runs the same phase together before advancing to the next.

How the cycle self-adjusts

When a client completes all four phases they have completed one full training block. On week five — the start of the second block — they return to the Strength phase and perform AMRAP test sets to reassess their 1RM. If the client can perform more reps or lift more weight than the previous block, their estimated 1RM increases and all assigned weights recalibrate accordingly for the following three phases. The program progresses with the client based on their performance.

Progressive overload by design

The four-phase cycle is not arbitrary. Each phase creates a different physiological stimulus — strength, hypertrophy at moderate volume, hypertrophy at higher volume, and peak volume with intensity techniques. By rotating through all four, the program ensures the client's body is never fully adapted to a single stimulus, and progression is built into the structure rather than left to guesswork.

Deviating from the cycle

Client injury or acute fatigue may warrant skipping a high-intensity phase. When this occurs, document the deviation in the session log and resume the cycle at the correct point in the next session.

04

The One Rep Max Foundation

All training weights at Kalos are derived from the client's one rep max (1RM) for each exercise. This is not a guideline — it is the mechanism that makes progression objective and eliminates guesswork. The 1RM is the anchor for every intensity prescription across all three phases.

Establishing the initial 1RM

For a new client or a new exercise, the 1RM is established through a standard testing protocol or calculated using the Epley formula from a submaximal set. The trainer records this value in the client's profile before programming any subsequent sessions.

When to retest — the AMRAP test

You do not retest 1RM on a fixed schedule. You retest when you observe it. During the Strength Phase, watch the client's working sets on major compound exercises. If the first sets at the training weight look clean — good bar speed, solid form, reps completed with capacity remaining — the final working set becomes the AMRAP test.

AMRAP test protocol

On the final working set of every major compound exercise on Strength Day, if the preceding sets have looked strong, that last set becomes the AMRAP — the client performs as many reps as possible at the working weight rather than stopping at the target rep count.

Record the weight and the total reps completed. The platform calculates the new 1RM automatically. The result becomes the new 1RM for that exercise, and all future training weights are recalculated from it.

If the working sets looked labored or the client flagged fatigue, do not call the test. Stay at current weights and reassess at the next Strength Phase.

Epley formula reference

The Kalos platform calculates 1RM automatically after every AMRAP test. Trainers do not need to run the numbers manually — but every trainer should understand the formula behind the calculation.

1RM = Weight × (1 + Reps ÷ 30)
Example: Client completes 8 reps at 80 kg.  1RM = 80 × (1 + 8 ÷ 30) = 80 × 1.267 = 101.3 kg
05

Session Protocol

Every Kalos session follows the same sequence. This is not a suggestion — it is the standard. Consistency of process is how we deliver consistency of results.

Before the client arrives

Review the client's session history. Know where they are in the macro-cycle, what weights they hit last session, and any notes from previous sessions.
Program the day's session. Determine every exercise, the target weight for each set, and the rep targets based on the current phase and the client's 1RM values. The session is planned before the client walks in.
Why setup matters

Setting up the next exercise during the client's rest is one of the most visible expressions of the Kalos standard. It signals to the client that you are always one step ahead — that their session is managed, their time is respected, and they never have to stand around waiting. For a premium client, this is the difference between a coach and a trainer.

The session opening

Greet the client and direct them to warm up. While they warm up, confirm how they are feeling — sleep, stress, soreness — and factor this into your real-time adjustments for the session.
Brief the session. Before the first working set, give the client a one-minute overview of what you are doing today, why, and what you are targeting. The client should always understand the purpose of the session.

During each exercise

Introduce the exercise. If the client is unfamiliar with the movement, demonstrate it and explain the target muscle group and the correct movement pattern before they attempt it.
Count every rep. Out loud. Give verbal encouragement to keep the client pushing through the set. Your voice is part of the service.
Watch and assess. While you count, you are evaluating. Is bar speed good or slow? Is form breaking down early? Does the client look well-rested or depleted? Your observation during each set determines your decision for the next one.
Adjust the weight for the next set. If the set looked strong and the client has capacity, increase. If the client is struggling or showing fatigue signals, decrease. The programmed weight is your starting point — your in-session observation is what makes it right for today.
Set up the next exercise. During the client's rest between sets, set up the next exercise. Load the bar, place the dumbbells, adjust the machine. The client's recovery time is your setup time — they move from one exercise to the next without waiting.
Log the set immediately. Record the exercise, weight, and reps in the client's session log before moving on. Accurate data is how progression is tracked and how the next session is programmed.

After the session

Put away all equipment. Re-rack every plate, return every dumbbell, and reset every machine or bench used during the session. The client leaves without touching a single piece of equipment.

A client should never have to clean up after their own session. Tidy equipment is part of the product — the same way a premium restaurant clears your table. It signals care, and it sets the standard for the next client walking in.

06

In-Session Judgment

The macro-cycle and 1RM percentages give you a plan. What you observe during the session determines whether you execute that plan, push beyond it, or pull back. This real-time judgment is the most skilled part of the work.

Signals to increase weight

Rep speed is fast and controlled throughout the set
The client completes the target reps with visible capacity remaining
Form does not break down at any point in the set
The client reports feeling well-rested and is showing up energized

Signals to maintain or reduce

Rep speed slows significantly in the final reps
Form begins to compromise before the target rep count is reached
The client reports poor sleep, high stress, or unusual fatigue
The client flags unusual soreness or discomfort in a working muscle
The principle of loaded attention

A client who trains at Kalos is paying for the luxury of not having to think. They should not be tracking their own weights, counting their own reps, or deciding whether to go heavier. That cognitive load belongs to you.

When a client finishes a set, your job is already done for the next one. The weight is adjusted. The plan is updated. They rest. You think.
